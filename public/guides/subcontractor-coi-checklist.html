An 8-minute guide for general contractors

The subcontractor certificate checklist

A certificate of insurance is a summary, not coverage. The ACORD 25 itself says it "confers no rights upon the certificate holder." What protects you is in the policy and the endorsements attached to it. Here's what to check on every certificate your subs send, in the order we check it.

1. Page one: the ACORD 25

Start with the form everyone already looks at, but read every box, not just the dates.

  • Named insured matches your subcontract. The legal entity on the certificate should be the same one that signed your subcontract. "Lone Pine Framing" and "Lone Pine Framing LLC" are not the same insured.
  • General liability is written on an occurrence basis. Claims-made policies only respond while the policy is still in force, which is a problem for claims that show up years after the job.
  • Each-occurrence and aggregate limits meet your contract. $1M / $2M is common. Check the products-completed operations aggregate too.
  • The aggregate applies per project, if your contract asks for it. A per-policy aggregate is shared across every job the sub works, so one bad claim elsewhere can use up the limit that was meant for yours.
  • Auto, umbrella and workers' comp are all listed, with employers' liability limits (each accident, disease each employee, disease policy limit).
  • The "ADDL INSD" and "SUBR WVD" boxes are checked. Treat these as a hint, not proof. The form states that additional insured status requires the policy to be endorsed.
  • Dates. Every policy should run past today, and ideally past the end of the sub's work on your job.

2. The endorsement pages

This is where most gaps hide, because spreadsheets don't track it and the pages are often missing from the PDF entirely. Ask for the actual endorsement pages, not a description of them.

CG 20 10
Additional insured, ongoing operations. Covers you while the sub's work is in progress. It may be scheduled (your company named on the form) or blanket (automatic status "when required by written contract", such as CG 20 38 or CG 20 33).
CG 20 37
Additional insured, completed operations. Covers you for claims that arise after the work is finished: water intrusion, structural defects, a failed connection found two years later. This is the endorsement most often missing.
CG 20 01
Primary and noncontributory. Makes the sub's policy respond first, before yours is touched. Without it, your own carrier and loss history absorb part of a loss the sub caused.
CG 24 04
Waiver of subrogation, general liability. Stops the sub's carrier from suing you to recover what it paid.
WC 00 03 13
Waiver of subrogation, workers' comp. The same protection on the workers' comp policy, where many jobsite injury claims start.
CA 20 48 / CA 04 44
Auto designated insured and auto waiver. Extends the sub's auto policy to you, and waives recovery against you, when your contract requires it.

Edition dates matter. Additional insured forms from the 04 13 edition onward limit coverage to what your written contract requires, so your subcontract's insurance clause has to ask for it specifically. Blanket forms usually also require that written contract to be signed before the work starts.

3. Exclusions that quietly remove coverage

A certificate can show full limits while the policy behind it excludes exactly the work the sub is doing for you. Watch for:

  • Contractual liability limitation (CG 21 39). Removes coverage for the liability the sub agreed to take on in your contract, which is the point of your indemnity clause.
  • Classification limitations. The policy only covers the operations listed on it. A sub classified as "carpentry" doing roofing work may have no coverage at all for that work.
  • Residential, height, EIFS or subsidence exclusions, when your project involves any of them.
  • Action-over exclusions, which drop coverage when the sub's own injured employee sues you. These matter most in New York.

4. Workers' compensation

  • The states where your job is located are covered (item 3.A on the policy's information page).
  • Watch for "ghost" policies. A sole proprietor can buy a minimum-premium policy that covers no employees. If that sub shows up with a crew, nobody on that crew is covered.
  • Exemption certificates vary by state and often don't protect you as the hiring contractor.

5. Keeping it current

A certificate is only right on the day it was issued. Ask brokers for renewal certificates 30 days before each expiration, keep every version with the date you received it, and keep the endorsement pages with each one. At your annual premium audit, your carrier will look for proof that every sub was insured while working for you. Where it can't find it, the sub's payroll or contract cost can be added to your own premium.
