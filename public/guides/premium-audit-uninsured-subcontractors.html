A 6-minute guide for general contractors

Premium audits and uninsured subcontractors

Your general liability and workers’ comp premiums are estimates until the policy year ends. Then your carrier audits what actually happened. When you can’t show that a sub was insured for the whole time they worked for you, the carrier can treat that sub’s work as yours and charge you premium for it.

1. How the audit works

After each policy period, an auditor reviews your books: payroll, sales or receipts, and what you paid each subcontractor (often from your 1099s and accounts payable). For every sub, they’ll ask for a certificate of insurance showing coverage during the dates that sub worked.

  • A current certificate isn’t enough. It has to cover the period the work was done. A certificate that lapsed mid-job leaves a gap for the rest of the job.
  • The names have to match. The insured on the certificate should be the same entity you paid.
  • Limits can matter too. Many carriers expect a sub’s limits to be at least what your policy requires. A sub with lower limits may be treated as uninsured.

2. What it costs on general liability

Payments to a sub without acceptable coverage are usually added to your exposure under a subcontractor classification, rated per $1,000 of cost. The rate comes from your own policy, and it is typically much higher than the rate for insured subcontracted work.

Illustrative example

You paid a framing sub $200,000 during the year and can’t produce a certificate covering their work. If your policy rates uninsured subcontracted work at $15 per $1,000, the audit adds $3,000 to your general liability premium, before any workers’ comp charge.

Numbers are for illustration only. Your actual classes and rates are on your policy’s rating pages; ask your broker.

3. What it costs on workers’ comp

In most states, if a sub has no workers’ comp, their workers can be treated as your employees for an injury claim. At audit, the carrier can add the labor portion of what you paid that sub (or the whole amount, if labor isn’t broken out) to your own payroll, at the rate for the work they did. For trades like roofing, that rate can be several times your office payroll rate.

4. How to prepare

  • Collect a certificate before a sub starts work, not when the auditor asks.
  • Collect renewals before policies expire, so coverage is continuous for the whole job.
  • Keep every version with the date you received it, together with the endorsement pages.
  • Track payments by sub so you can match each 1099 to its certificates.
  • Check limits and workers’ comp, not just whether a certificate exists. Our certificate checklist covers what to look for.
